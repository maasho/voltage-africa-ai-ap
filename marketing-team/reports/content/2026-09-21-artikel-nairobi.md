Ask three Nairobi installers what a solar system costs and you will get three very different numbers — anything from "around sixty thousand" to "just over a million". Both can be honest answers. They are simply describing different systems. This guide gives you real price ranges from the Kenyan market, explains exactly what moves the number up or down, and shows you how to compare quotes without getting burned.

## The short answer: indicative price ranges

First, the honest disclaimer: there is no official price list, and every property is different. The figures below are **indicative ranges published by Kenyan solar companies in 2025 and 2026 — orientation, not quotations**:

| System type | Indicative installed price (KES) |
|---|---|
| Basic backup / lighting setup | from ~55,000 |
| Small residential (1–3 kW) | ~70,000–300,000 |
| 3 kW complete system | ~480,000–650,000 |
| 5 kW hybrid with battery storage | ~750,000–1,050,000 |
| 7 kW system | ~1,150,000–1,500,000 |
| 10 kW system | ~1,600,000–2,100,000 |

Sources: Green Camel Ltd (2026, basic setups from ~KSh 55,000); Suntech Power KE via LinkedIn (2025, 1–3 kW at KES 70,000–300,000); SolarCity Eco Energies (August 2025, 3–10 kW ranges). You will also see budget off-grid packages advertised around KSh 150,000 for a 3 kW label (e.g. Solarsasa, Nairobi area) — which usually means smaller batteries and entry-level components. The spread is the point: read on.

## Why two "5 kW systems" can differ by half a million shillings

Five factors do almost all the damage — or the saving:

**1. The battery bank.** This is the single largest cost driver. Lithium (LiFePO4) batteries cost significantly more upfront than lead-acid but deliver far more charge cycles and deeper usable capacity, so the cost per stored kilowatt-hour over their lifetime is usually lower. A quote with a small lead-acid bank and a quote with 10 kWh of lithium are different products wearing the same "5 kW" label.

**2. The inverter.** Tier-1 hybrid inverters with proper local warranty support cost more than grey-import units — and the difference shows up the first time something fails.

**3. Your roof and site.** Roof structure, cable runs, shading work, earthing and surge protection all add labour and materials. Installation labour alone typically runs **KSh 15,000–50,000** for a home system, depending on complexity (Source: Green Camel Ltd, 2026).

**4. Component brands.** Quality monocrystalline panels in Kenya average roughly **KSh 90–110 per watt** (Source: Green Camel Ltd, 2026). Panels dramatically below that deserve hard questions.

**5. What's included.** Commissioning, protection devices, a changeover switch, documentation, after-sales support — cheap quotes quietly strip these out.

## First, know your number: kWp vs kWh

Quotes only make sense once you know your daily consumption. **kWp** is the size label of the panel array — peak power under ideal sun. **kWh** is the energy your household actually uses per day, and what the system must deliver. A typical Nairobi home running lights, TV, fridge, Wi-Fi and phone charging lands somewhere in the single-digit to low-teens kWh per day — but measure, don't guess: a week of reading your meter or a proper load audit beats any rule of thumb. Our [solar sizing guide](guide-solar-sizing-nigeria.html) walks through the load-audit maths step by step (written for Nigeria; the principles apply directly in Kenya).

## Non-negotiable in Kenya: the EPRA licence

Kenya regulates solar work properly — use that. Under the Energy Act 2019, solar PV installation must be carried out by licensed technicians or under their supervision, and the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority (**EPRA**) issues licences by class: **T1** for small DC systems up to 100 Wp, **T2** for medium systems with multiple batteries and an inverter, and **T3** for advanced projects including grid-connected and hybrid systems. Companies need their own classes — **C1** for design-and-install contractors (which requires a T3-licensed technician on staff), plus V1/V2 for vendors and manufacturers/importers (Sources: kienergytech.com EPRA licence guide, July 2026; EPRA Electricity and Renewable Energy Licensing Application Guide, epra.go.ke).

Ask for the licence class and number, and check it against EPRA's public register. It takes two minutes and filters out a large share of problems before they start.

## What a serious quote looks like

A professional Nairobi quote is not a WhatsApp message with a single number. It follows a **load audit** — the installer lists your appliances, their wattage and hours of use, and asks which loads can shift to daylight. Only then comes an itemised document: panel brand and wattage, inverter brand, battery type and usable kWh, protection equipment, mounting, labour, and warranty terms for each component. No audit, no quote — treat that as a rule. And compare at least three quotes: in any market, that habit alone routinely saves buyers a five-figure shilling amount.

## Does it pay back? The honest logic

We won't quote you a payback period — anyone who does without seeing your Kenya Power bills and your load profile is guessing. The logic, though, is solid: solar has high upfront cost and very low running cost, so your payback depends on three things — how much grid electricity (at your tariff band) the system displaces, how much generator or outage cost it removes, and how long the battery lasts before replacement. Owners of well-sized systems commonly report electricity bill reductions in the range of 60–90 % (Source: SolarCity Eco Energies owner survey summary, 2025). Get those three numbers for *your* home and the maths does itself.

## Red flags: when "cheap" is expensive

- A quote **far below** the ranges above — almost always undersized batteries, unbranded components, or both.
- No EPRA licence number, or "my cousin's company is licensed".
- No load audit — the installer never asked what you actually run.
- "550W panel, 5kW inverter" with no brand names.
- Large upfront cash payment with no written, itemised contract.
